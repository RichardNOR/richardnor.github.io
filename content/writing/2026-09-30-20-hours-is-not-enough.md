There's a popular book called *The First 20 Hours* by Josh Kaufman. The idea is that 20 hours of focused practice is enough to get past the frustrating beginner stage of almost any skill. It's a good book, and I think he's right about that narrow claim. Twenty hours is enough to stop being terrible at something.

But I don't think 20 hours gets you to good. I think it gets you to "not bad." And there's a big difference between the two.

## The part 20 hours doesn't cover

I've noticed that there is a real gap between being able to do something alone at my desk and being able to do it in front of other people. Explaining a dashboard to a manager. Walking through a SQL query in an interview. Answering a question I didn't prepare for.

That second kind of skill needs something 20 hours can't give you, which is internal confidence. And confidence doesn't come from reading or watching. It comes from repetition and from feedback loops, especially in person, where someone pushes back on your work and you have to respond in real time without a safety net.

For me that takes closer to 50 to 100 hours. Not 20. Some skills need less and some need way more, but for most things worth learning, 20 is just the entry fee.

## Skills stack on top of each other

Here's the part that actually changed how I think about this. Skills compound. The earlier you put in your 50 or 100 hours on one skill, the more you can use it as leverage for the next one.

Say someone learns to code early. Then they pick up some marketing and sales. Then they learn SQL. None of those alone is a career. But together, that person could build a database driven marketing service for small businesses. Each skill gave them a new angle on the last one.

That's why I don't think of the first 100 hours as a cost. I think of it as the foundation the next skill sits on. If you rush the first one, the second one wobbles.

## What I'm doing with this

I'm treating this as my method going forward. When I pick up a new skill, I'm not judging myself at 20 hours. I'm giving it 50 at minimum before I decide if I'm any good, and I'm building in ways to practice in front of other people, not just alone at my desk.

I still think Kaufman's framework is useful. Break the skill down. Learn just enough to catch your own mistakes. Remove the friction that stops you from practicing. I just think the number at the end is bigger than he says. And I think the sooner you accept that, the sooner the hours start working for you instead of against you.
